0.1 How to read this book

Every component section has the same eight parts, in the same order. If you are implementing, you can read parts 3, 5 and 8 and ignore the rest.

#PartWhat it is for
1Purpose and boundaryWhat it is, and which component to use instead when it is the wrong one. Boundaries are why we ended up with three modal systems.
2AnatomyNamed parts. The names are the class names.
3Token map by roleEvery part, its token, what it resolves to, and its measured contrast against its own surface. Normative.
4Required statesVariants and interaction states. If it is listed here, shipping without it is incomplete, not minimal.
5Accessibility — hard rulesNumbered MUST / MUST NOT. Each one is testable by a human in under ten seconds.
6BehaviourTiming, stacking, dismissal, focus movement, announcement.

8Markup contractThe DOM that Vue 2 and React must both produce. This is what makes the spec framework-agnostic and what makes conformance greppable.
Why a rendered example and a markup contract, rather than a component library

A library commits to a framework and dates immediately — Unity is Vue 2, Scale targets React. A contract does not. Both implementations produce the same DOM and consume the same tokens, so a tenant stylesheet, a QA script and a designer's eye all target one thing. The library follows the contract; the contract does not follow the library.

0.2 The four laws

Four rules govern every component in this book. Everything downstream is application. Laws 1 and 2 are locked outputs of the conformance audit. Laws 3 and 4 are what the audit numbers force once you try to apply 1 and 2 to real components.

Law 1
Fill, text and border are different steps of one colour. A hex is never reused across roles.

One value cannot satisfy three contrast obligations at once. The audit found the current skins using the same state hex as background 1,289 times, as text 1,193 times and as border 593 times — which is why warning sits at 1.81:1 and success at 3.39:1.

Accent step 11 is hand-corrected to #006c78. The generator produced #007d8b, which broke monotonicity — lighter than step 10, so the ramp reversed. The corrected value is OKLCH L 0.486 (C 0.084, H 208.2°), sitting between step 10 (L 0.509) and step 12 (L 0.334): lightness now descends monotonically, hue holds within ~1°, chroma tapers toward 12. 6.15:1 on white. Documented as hand-tuned at step 11, not generator output — re-apply on any regenerate.

Law 2
A state is a set of roles, not a colour. It resolves to four ramp positions from one hue, contrast-correct by construction.

Surface, border, icon, text. Four roles, one hue, no judgement calls at the call site. A developer choosing a state never chooses a value.

Law 3
Contrast is a property of a pair, not of a step. Every token declares the surface it is legal against.

This is the correction that makes Law 1 survive contact with a tinted surface. "Step 11 is accessible text" is true on white and false on a state surface: on step 3, the three state step-11s measure 4.33, 4.05 and 4.42:1 — all under AA. On a tinted surface, text moves to step 12. See 0.5.

The same applies to borders. Accent step 7 on white is 1.80:1, so it is a legal container hairline and an illegal focus ring or sole state indicator, both of which owe 3:1.

Law 4
Step 9 has no role in a state-bearing surface. There is no solid state fill.

White text on step 9 measures 3.87:1 on error, 3.15:1 on success and 2.04:1 on warning. Three of four fail AA. Making solid state surfaces safe requires a per-hue on-fill token, which reintroduces exactly the judgement call Law 2 removes — and the audit shows what happens to judgement calls at 62,235 call sites.

So: no solid alerts, no solid toasts, no solid banners. Step 9 stays where it earns its contrast — accent fills and primary actions on white. Hue in the state group is carried by surface, border and icon.

Status and state are different things and must not share a namespace

Scale already ships --status-active-*, --status-pending-*, --status-cancelled-*. Those describe a property of a record — a job is on hold, a review is pending. They persist, they are filterable, they belong to data.

This book's --state-* tokens describe a system response to an event — the save failed, the export finished. They are ephemeral and belong to the interface.

They currently collide on hue: green means both "complete" and "success", amber means both "pending" and "warning". That collision is survivable, but sharing a token namespace is not — it guarantees someone eventually styles a toast with --status-cancelled-bg and nobody catches it in review. Keep the two vocabularies separate and the ambiguity stays visible.

0.3 Token layers and reach

Three layers. The rule that matters is not what each layer contains, it is how far into the stack each layer is allowed to reach.

LayerContainsMay referenceMay be referenced by
1 · Primitive--accent-9, --amber-11Nothing. Literal values.Layer 2 only.
2 · Semantic--state-warning-icon, --text-defaultLayer 1.Layer 3, and application CSS.
3 · Component--alert-surface, --modal-backdropLayer 2. Never layer 1.The component's own rules only.

A component stylesheet that references a primitive is non-conformant, even when the value is right. This is the single check with the highest leverage in the book: it is one grep, it has no false negatives, and it catches the failure mode the audit found in the chatbot widget — a step 11 value pinned into the step 9 slot, where the instinct was correct, the slot was wrong, and the review passed because the value looked reasonable.

Layer 3 is not optional decoration

Variants set component tokens and nothing else. The alert has one background declaration in the entire component; the four variants each set four custom properties. That is what makes a fifth state a four-line change instead of a new rule block, and it is why the audit's finding that no overlay has any state variant is a cheap fix rather than a rewrite — once the layer exists.

0.7 Conformance method

Every component section ends with checks that a script can run. This is the part that closes the loop: the audit that produced this book took six scripted passes over 890,000 lines. The next one should be a CI job.

A rule that cannot be grepped is a preference. If a rule in this book has no check, either it needs one or it does not belong in the book.

CheckPassing result
Layer violationNo component stylesheet references a layer 1 primitive.
Literal violationNo hex literal from 0.5 appears outside the token declaration block.
Role violationNo --state-*-surface in a color:; no --state-*-text in a background:.
Contract violationEvery .sd-alert carries data-state. Every dismiss control has an accessible name.
AdoptionCount of legacy calls remaining. Conformance and adoption are different numbers and are reported separately.

Two phases, tagged per check. This book specs the finished component — colour, behaviour and accessibility as one thing — because it describes the destination a React build aims at. The near-term pass is UI-only: colour and tokens in the existing codebase, no behaviour change. So every conformance check below carries a phase tag. UI is satisfiable now, as colour/token conformance, without touching behaviour or flow. React requires the component to be built or replaced and rides the module's React migration. A surface that passes its UI checks and fails its React checks is visually conformant with its behaviour still pending — the expected interim state, not a defect. Colour-conformant is not component-conformant.

Conformance is not adoption

The audit's central finding was that sd-modal is used 5 times against 1,484 legacy calls. A component can be perfectly conformant and reach nobody. Every section reports both numbers, because the remediation for each is completely different: conformance is a pull request, adoption is a programme.

1.1 Inline alert

The base component of the state-bearing group. Toast and banner are this plus ephemerality and this plus persistence — so this is where the state model is settled.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A message bound to a region of the page, in the document flow, persisting until the condition that caused it changes. It does not move, it does not time out, and it does not take focus.

Use an inline alert whenUse something else when
The message is about a specific region and belongs beside itThe message is about a background operation the user has moved on from → Toast
The user must still be able to see and act on the underlying contentThe message is about the whole application or account and outlives the page → Banner
The condition persists until something changes itThe message blocks progress and needs a decision now → Modal
The message can carry a link or a secondary actionThe message is about one specific field → field-level validation, not this

Field-level validation is the boundary most often crossed. An alert summarising six invalid fields is correct; an alert standing in for the message under the field is not, and it costs the user the pointer to where the problem is.

Two decisions in that table are worth stating out loud.

Links and actions take body colour, not icon colour. Every instinct says a link should be the accent or the state hue. On a tinted surface both fail: step 11 lands at 4.05:1 on warning. The link is distinguished by underline and weight, which is a stronger affordance than hue anyway and survives the greyscale test.

Dismiss hover is a neutral alpha overlay, not a state step. Using a state step would need four more tokens and four more measurements to add nothing. The overlay works identically on all four surfaces and on any fifth we add later.

Known asymmetry — the info icon is darker than the other three

Info's icon resolves to corrected accent step 11 at 7.77:1, against 4.05–4.42:1 for the other three, so it reads visually heavier. This is downstream of the same dark anchor that broke the ramp. Using accent step 9 for the info icon would balance it at 5.20:1 — but it puts step 9 back in the state group and reopens Law 4 for one glyph's sake. Recommendation: keep step 11 and accept the asymmetry. Flagged so it is recognised as a known trade rather than rediscovered as a bug.

5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST resolve every colour through --alert-*. A variant sets four custom properties and declares no other colour. MUST NOT reference a layer 1 primitive anywhere in component CSS.
  2. MUST carry an icon and a text message. Hue is never the sole carrier of state (WCAG 1.4.1). The alert must survive a greyscale screenshot.
  3. MUST mark the icon aria-hidden="true" and convey state in text — either in the copy or in a visually hidden prefix. A screen reader user gets "Error:", not a described glyph.
  4. MUST use step 12 for all text on the alert surface. MUST NOT use step 11 for body copy, links or action labels. 4.05:1 fails AA.
  5. MUST NOT render a solid state fill or place white text on any state step 9. Law 4.
  6. MUST announce dynamically inserted alerts via a live region: role="alert" for error and warning, role="status" for success and info. MUST NOT put a live region on an alert that is present at page load — it is read in document order, and a live region makes it interrupt or duplicate.
  7. MUST keep the live region container in the DOM before the message is inserted. A live region added and populated in the same tick is not announced in most screen readers.
  8. MUST keep the focus ring at 2px accent-9, 2px offset, on :focus-visible. MUST NOT replace it with a background change — the offset ring is the only indicator that clears 3:1 on all four tinted surfaces.
  9. MUST NOT place a filled button inside an alert. Actions are underlined text buttons. A filled button needs an on-fill colour, which reopens Law 4, and it competes with the page's one primary action.
  10. MUST NOT offer dismissal on an error whose condition is unresolved. Dismissing an alert the user cannot act on hides a live problem and leaves no way to recover it. Errors bound to a persistent condition are not dismissible.
  11. MUST give the dismiss control a specific accessible name — "Dismiss error message", not "Close" — and a 44×44 interactive area via pseudo-element, not padding.
  12. MUST move focus to a sensible destination when a focused alert is removed: the triggering control if it still exists, otherwise the container heading. MUST NOT let focus fall to <body>.

6 · Behaviour

  • Lifetime. Until the condition changes. No timers.
  • Placement. Directly above the region it describes, inside that region's bounds. A form alert sits inside the form.
  • Stacking. Maximum two per region. A third means the messages should be one alert with a list.
  • Replacement. A new alert in the same slot replaces rather than appends, and re-announces.
  • Entry. 150ms opacity fade. No slide, no height animation — inline alerts change layout, and animating that costs a reflow and a jump under the pointer.

Why data-state and not a modifier class

A class is additive: sd-alert-error sd-alert-warning is valid HTML and renders whichever rule wins the cascade. An attribute holds one value. The state becomes an enumerable, greppable, mutually exclusive property that a linter can validate against a list of four — and it prevents the specific defect the audit found, where the overlay system has no state layer and the toast classes that do exist are applied without ever being styled.

1.2 Toast

The inline alert plus ephemerality. Same state model, same four tokens, same contrast — lifted out of the document flow, elevated over content, announcing a background operation the user has already moved on from, then leaving on its own.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A transient message about an operation that completed or failed away from the user's current focus. It floats above content, times out, and never blocks progress. The test is single: if dismissing it loses something the user still needs, it should not have been a toast.

Use a toast whenUse something else when
A background operation finished and the user has moved on from where it startedThe message is about a region still on screen and belongs beside it → Inline alert
The confirmation would otherwise be invisible — a save, a queued export, a copied linkLosing the message strands the user — they must act on it in place → Inline alert or Modal
The message is one line, at most with a single recoverable actionThe message needs a title, body, and two actions → Inline alert
The condition is a moment, not a stateThe condition persists and must stay visible until resolved → Banner

The boundary most often crossed is the error toast. An error the user must fix is not a background event: putting it in a surface that times out means the one message they needed vanishes in five seconds. A toast may report a background failure — "Export failed" with a retry — but the instant recovery has to happen in place, it is an alert or a modal. This is why the timing table below never auto-dismisses error or warning.

A toast is always inserted dynamically, so — unlike the alert — there is no page-load case to suppress: a toast always announces. The severity split drives two things at once: which live region it enters, and whether it is allowed to time out. Both are covered in Behaviour.

Interaction states: default; hover and :focus-within, which both pause the timer; dismiss hover and active; :focus-visible on the dismiss and on the single action. There is no quiet toast — a toast is never nested inside a bordered container — and no disabled toast.

5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST resolve every colour through --toast-*. A variant sets four custom properties and nothing else. MUST NOT reference a layer 1 primitive in component CSS.
  2. MUST carry an icon and a text message. Hue is never the sole carrier of state (WCAG 1.4.1). The toast survives a greyscale screenshot.
  3. MUST render the region container in the DOM at page load, empty, before any toast is inserted. A live region created and populated in the same tick is not announced in most screen readers. This is the most common toast failure and the audit's B3 is its symptom — three toast classes are set as class names with no region and no styling behind them.
  4. MUST split politeness by severity across two regions: error and warning into an assertive region (aria-live="assertive"), success and info into a polite one (aria-live="polite"). One shared region forces a choice between interrupting the user for a success or under-announcing an error. Two regions cost nothing and remove the compromise.
  5. MUST NOT auto-dismiss error or warning. A message that announces assertively and then removes itself before it can be read or acted on is worse than silence. Error and warning persist until dismissed.
  6. MUST pause every timer on hover and on :focus-within, and resume on leave (WCAG 2.2.1). A user reading the line or reaching for undo must not have it disappear under the pointer. A toast carrying an action holds a minimum of 8s regardless, so the action is reachable.
  7. MUST keep the toast keyboard-reachable for as long as it is visible: the dismiss and the single action are in the tab order, and dismissing returns focus to where it was. MUST NOT let focus fall to <body>.
  8. MUST use step 12 for text and the 2px accent-9 offset focus ring — identical to the alert, for the identical reason: the ring is the only indicator that clears 3:1 on all four tinted surfaces.
  9. MUST NOT carry more than one action, and that action is non-destructive and recoverable. A toast is the wrong surface for a decision — it times out, and a decision that expires is a trap.
  10. MUST NOT render a solid state fill or place white text on any state step 9. Law 4. The vendor default the audit leaves in place does exactly this — see the rejected example.

6 · Behaviour

The alert's behaviour is one line: stay until the condition changes. The toast's behaviour is the set of rules for leaving, and that is what earns it a section.

  • Stacking. Newest at the anchored edge; maximum three visible; a fourth queues and enters as one leaves. More than three at once is a symptom — batch them, or the operation should have reported inline.
  • Order carries no priority. Toasts enter at the region's edge and push the stack. Severity is carried by state and announcement, never by position — a user must never have to infer importance from where a toast sits.
  • Portal and layering. Every toast renders into the single region, above app chrome but below modals. A modal owns the screen while it blocks on a decision; a toast floating over it competes for attention it should not have.
  • Motion. Enter is a 150ms fade with a short translate along the anchored axis; exit is a 150ms fade. Under prefers-reduced-motion, opacity only — no translate. Never animate height: toasts are portalled, so there is no reflow to justify it, and a height transition makes the rest of the stack jump.
  • Rapid succession. Identical consecutive toasts collapse into one with a count rather than announcing the same string five times into a live region. The wrapper owns this — which is the argument for the wrapper being a real chokepoint, below.

1.3 Banner

The inline alert plus persistence. Same state model, same four tokens, same contrast — full-bleed across the top of the application, outliving the page that raised it, speaking for the whole product or account rather than one region. It is the last of the state-bearing group, and it is the one under the most pressure to break Law 4.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A message about the application or the account that persists across navigation until the condition it reports is resolved. It is anchored to the top of the app frame, spans the full width, and is the most prominent surface a non-blocking message can occupy. That prominence is the whole reason it exists — and the whole reason it is the easiest component to misuse.

Use a banner whenUse something else when
The message is about the whole application or account — billing, an outage, a maintenance window, a plan limitThe message is about a region on the current page → Inline alert
It must survive navigation between pages, not just re-render on oneIt is a moment, not a state — a save, a completed export → Toast
The user should see it but is not blocked from workingThe user cannot safely proceed until they act → Modal
Exactly one condition qualifies at app levelSeveral page regions each have something to say → Inline alerts, in place

The boundary most often crossed is prominence-seeking: a page-level message promoted to a banner because it feels important. Importance is not the test — scope is. A banner is for what is true everywhere in the app. If navigating away should make the message go, it was never a banner.

5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST resolve every colour through --banner-*. A variant sets four custom properties and nothing else. MUST NOT reference a layer 1 primitive in component CSS.
  2. MUST carry an icon and a text message. Hue is never the sole carrier of state (WCAG 1.4.1). Survives greyscale.
  3. MUST NOT render a solid state fill or white text on any state step 9 — the decision above, as a greppable rule. The banner surface is step 3; if a background resolves to step 9, it fails.
  4. MUST NOT use role="banner". That ARIA landmark is the page masthead; putting it on a state banner creates a second banner landmark and breaks landmark navigation. Use a labelled region — role="region" with an aria-label — or a plain element in document order.
  5. MUST use step 12 for text, links and the action label. MUST NOT use step 11 for body copy — 4.05:1 fails AA on the tinted surface.
  6. MUST announce a banner inserted after load through a live region — aria-live="assertive" for error/warning, polite for info/success. MUST NOT put a live region on a banner present at page load; it is read in document order.
  7. MUST place the banner first in the document, before the nav rail and main, so it is encountered before the content it qualifies — and keep it in the tab order ahead of the rail.
  8. MUST keep the focus ring at 2px accent-9, 2px offset, on :focus-visible — the one indicator that clears 3:1 on all four surfaces.
  9. MUST NOT place a filled button in a banner. One underlined text action. A filled button needs an on-fill colour, which reopens Law 4, and competes with the page's single primary action.
  10. MUST NOT offer dismissal on an error bound to an unresolved account condition, and MUST remember every dismissal it does offer, per user, so a dismissed banner does not return on the next navigation — the behaviour that makes users install banner-blockers.

6 · Behaviour

The alert stays until its condition changes on one page. The banner does the same thing across pages — which turns persistence, precedence and placement into real rules.

  • Persistence. The banner's state lives above the router, not in a page. It renders identically on every route until the condition clears, and a client-side navigation never re-animates or re-announces it.
  • Precedence — one at a time. At most one banner is visible. If more than one condition qualifies, the highest severity wins: error > warning > info > success. Two genuinely co-equal app-level conditions is the signal that one of them is not actually app-scoped and belongs in an alert.
  • Placement. Full-bleed across the very top of the app frame, above both the nav rail and the page content — it speaks for the whole product, so it sits above the whole product. It pushes content down; it never overlays, because a banner does not block and must not cover.
  • Dismissal memory. A dismissed advisory banner stays dismissed for that user until the condition changes state — stored by a stable id for the condition, not re-shown on reload or navigation.
  • Motion. On first appearance, a 150ms height-and-fade in. On dismissal, a 150ms collapse. Under prefers-reduced-motion, opacity only. Never re-animate on navigation — the banner was already there.

2.1 Modal

The first overlay and the highest-stakes section in the book. It has to be good enough to justify replacing 1,484 legacy calls across two systems — and it is the one component where a filled primary button is allowed, which makes it the one place the book has to define an on-fill colour without reopening Law 4. It is sequenced here, after the state-bearing group, so the state layer it borrows already exists.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A surface that takes over the screen to get a decision or complete a focused task that must finish or be abandoned before the user does anything else. It blocks. That is its whole reason to exist and the whole reason it is overused: blocking is powerful, and power invites reaching for it when a quieter surface would do.

Use a modal whenUse something else when
The user must decide or finish before continuing, and proceeding without deciding would be wrongThe message is information the user can read and move past → Inline alert or Toast

Bringing those 5 into conformance changes nothing anyone sees. Decision 1 — a modal is neutral. State goes inside it, never on the frame.

There is no error modal, no success modal, no red or green dialog. A modal is --surface-default, always. When the task carries state — a destructive warning, a validation summary — that state is an inline alert inside the body, which is exactly what 1.1 is for. Tinting the whole dialog spends the largest state surface in the app to say what one alert says better, and it forces an on-fill contrast problem on every word of chrome. The frame is neutral; the state lives in the content.

Decision 2 — the one filled primary, and why it is allowed here when it is banned everywhere else

The state-bearing group forbids filled buttons because a filled button needs an on-fill colour, and on a state surface that means white on step 9 — Law 4. The modal is the exception, and it is a principled one: its primary is the accent, not a state, and --accent-9 carries white at 4.61:1, clearing AA. Law 4 governs the state families; the action colour is not one of them. So the modal gets exactly one filled button — the accent primary — and every other action in the footer is secondary. One filled button per modal, no exceptions: a second filled button is two primaries, which is no primary.

Decision 3 — the destructive primary, reconciling the flagged button

The obvious destructive button — white on --error-9 — is 3.87:1. It fails AA, and it is the exact defect the audit flagged against the current destructive button. White on error-9 fails, error-10 lands at 4.48 — a technical fail — and white on error-11 (#cf3716) is 4.99:1. So the destructive fill darkens to step 11. It is implemented the way every variant in this book is: data-intent="destructive" re-points one token (--overlay-primary → --action-danger) and nothing else. 5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST use role="dialog" with aria-modal="true", aria-labelledby pointing at the title and aria-describedby at the body. A modal with no accessible name is an unlabelled trap.
  2. MUST move focus into the dialog on open — to the first input, or to the safe action on a destructive modal — and MUST return focus to the triggering control on close. MUST NOT let focus land on <body>.
  3. MUST trap focus within the dialog while open: Tab and Shift+Tab cycle inside it and never reach the page behind the scrim.
  4. MUST lock background scroll while open and restore the exact scroll position on close, without the layout shift a naive overflow:hidden causes.
  5. MUST close on Escape for any dismissible modal. MUST NOT close on Escape when the modal is pending, or guards unsaved work — in which case Escape routes to the same confirm path as the close button.
  6. MUST carry exactly one filled primary in the footer. MUST NOT render two filled buttons. Secondary actions are outlined or text.
  7. MUST resolve the primary fill through --action-primary / --action-danger. MUST NOT place white on --error-9, or tint the surface with any state colour. Decisions 1 and 3.
  8. MUST NOT dismiss a decision-required modal by backdrop click. Backdrop click closes an advisory modal; a modal that demands a choice, is destructive, or holds unsaved input ignores it — an accidental click outside must not discard the decision.
  9. MUST NOT open a modal from a modal. Stacked dialogs bury focus, multiply the escape paths, and are the signal that the flow wanted a page. Replace the current modal's contents instead of stacking.
  10. MUST give the close control an accessible name — "Close dialog", not "Close" alone where ambiguous — and a 44×44 target via pseudo-element.

6 · Behaviour

  • Open. Scrim fades in over 150ms; the surface fades and scales from 98% to 100% over the same window. Focus moves in as rule 02 requires.
  • Dismissal. Three paths, and they are not equal: the close button and Escape always work on a dismissible modal; the backdrop works only on advisory ones (rule 08). A destructive or dirty modal has exactly one way out that isn't the action — Cancel — and it is the focused control.
  • Pending. On submit, the modal enters data-pending: the primary shows a spinner and locks, dismissal is suspended, and the result either closes the modal (success) or surfaces as an inline alert in the body (failure) — state inside the frame, per Decision 1.
  • Stacking. One modal at a time. A flow that seems to need a second modal replaces the first modal's content or becomes a page.
  • Motion. Under prefers-reduced-motion, scrim and surface fade only — no scale. Closing reverses the open in 120ms.

2.2 Drawer

The modal, re-anchored to an edge at full height. It inherits the entire 2.1 contract — scrim, focus trap, scroll lock, the three decisions, the one filled primary — unchanged. Almost nothing here is new colour or new rule. The section exists to make one call: when a drawer beats a modal, and when it is a modal wearing the wrong shape.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A full-height panel anchored to a viewport edge, for a focused task that needs more room than a centred dialog, or that benefits from staying spatially attached to the list it came from. It blocks, exactly as a modal does — the edge and the height are the only differences.

The drawer adds four.

  2. MUST NOT be used as a non-modal panel. A drawer has a scrim and a focus trap. If the design needs the page live underneath, that is the layout panel, not this component — do not strip the scrim off a drawer to fake it.
  3. MUST default to the right edge. Right matches reading order for a detail or edit task and is where users expect a panel opened from a row or item.
  4. MUST NOT anchor left where the left nav rail lives — a left drawer slides over the primary navigation, buries the user's orientation, and fights the one piece of chrome that should never move. Bottom is the mobile and compact-picker anchor; left is effectively unused.

6 · Behaviour

The decision this section exists to make — when a drawer beats a modal

Scope of task, not size of content. A modal is for a decision that should black out everything else: the answer is confirm or cancel, and what is behind is irrelevant to it, so centring it and dimming the rest is correct. A drawer is for a task — a form, an edit, a detail view — that needs room and benefits from staying attached to where it opened: you are editing this row, and the row's list is still there behind you, dimmed but oriented. A page is for a primary destination you navigate to. And the non-modal panel — the one this component is not — is for work you do alongside a live page. The failure mode in both directions: a one-line confirm stuffed into a full-height drawer (a modal wearing the wrong shape, displacing the whole edge for a yes/no), and a multi-field editor jammed into a centred modal that cannot hold it. Match the shape to the scope.

  • Anchoring. Right by default; bottom for mobile and compact contextual pickers; left avoided (rule 04). One drawer chooses one edge; a drawer does not move edges between opens.
  • Size. Side drawers step sm / md / lg by width and always run full height. A bottom drawer is content height, capped at 80vh, and scrolls its body past that.
  • Motion. Slides in from its anchored edge — translateX for side, translateY for bottom — over 200ms, while the scrim fades. Under prefers-reduced-motion, the scrim fades and the panel appears without translation. Closing reverses it.
  • Everything else is the modal's. Dismissal paths, focus management, scroll lock, the pending state and one-at-a-time stacking are inherited from 2.1 without change. A drawer does not open a drawer, and a drawer does not open a modal — that is a flow that wanted a page.

3.1 Tooltip

The smallest surface in the book and the one with the strictest rule. A short, non-interactive label anchored to a trigger — a name for an icon button, a hint, a fuller version of a truncated label. It is supplementary by definition: the moment a tooltip carries something the user needs to finish a task, it is the wrong component, and the whole section is built to hold that line.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A label that appears on hover and focus to add a word or a short phrase to a control that already works without it. It is text, it is brief, and it does nothing.

Use a tooltip whenUse something else when
An icon button needs a visible name, or a control needs a short hintThe content is required to use the control — a format rule, a validation constraint → inline text or a Popover
The information is genuinely optional — nice to have, not needed to proceedThe content is interactive — a link, a button, a field → Popover
A truncated label needs its full text on demandThe content is more than a phrase → Popover or a detail surface
The trigger's accessible name is already set elsewhereThe tooltip would be the control's only name → put the name in aria-label; the tooltip only echoes it

The one rule everything else hangs off: a tooltip is never load-bearing. Its content lives behind hover and focus, which means on touch it may never appear, and for a real share of keyboard and assistive-technology users it is missed. So anything a tooltip says must be true and available without it. If removing every tooltip would break a task, the tooltips were doing work they are not allowed to do — that work is inline text or a popover.

5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST NOT carry information required to complete a task. Everything a tooltip says is available without it. This is the rule the component exists to enforce; every rule below serves it.
  2. MUST appear on both hover and keyboard focus, and MUST NOT require a click or appear on hover alone. A tooltip that only shows on mouse hover is invisible to keyboard users.
  3. MUST satisfy WCAG 1.4.13 in full: dismissible — Escape hides it without moving the pointer; hoverable — the pointer can move onto the tooltip without it vanishing; persistent — it stays until dismissed or the trigger is left, never on a timeout.
  4. MUST keep the control's accessible name on the trigger, in aria-label, and reference the tooltip with aria-describedby. The tooltip is a visual echo of the name, not its only source. An icon button named only by its tooltip is nameless to touch, to screen readers that don't surface described-by on the way in, and to anyone whose pointer never rests.
  5. MUST NOT contain interactive content — no link, button, or field. Interactive content inside a surface that vanishes on pointer-leave is unusable. That is a popover.
  6. MUST treat touch as a case with no hover: assume the tooltip will not appear, and rely on aria-label and inline text to carry the meaning. A long-press reveal is a bonus, never the plan.
  7. MUST show one tooltip at a time, and hide on trigger activation — once the button is pressed, the action has happened and the label is moot.
  8. MUST stay within the viewport by flipping and shifting (Behaviour), never clipped at an edge or spilling off-screen.

6 · Behaviour

  • Positioning. Anchored to the trigger on the preferred side with a small offset; the arrow points at the trigger's centre.
  • Collision. If the preferred side would overflow the viewport, flip to the opposite side; if it would still overflow along the cross-axis, shift to stay in view. The arrow tracks the trigger through both.
  • Delay. Open after roughly 400ms so a pointer passing over a toolbar does not strobe tooltips; close almost immediately on leave, with a short grace period so the pointer can travel to a hoverable tooltip (rule 03). Once one tooltip has opened, a shared skip-delay window lets adjacent triggers open instantly while the user is scanning.
  • Trigger. Hover and focus open; leave, blur, Escape and activation close. Focus is a first-class trigger, not an afterthought.
  • Touch. No hover state, so in practice the tooltip does not fire; the meaning is already carried by the name and any inline text. The component degrades to nothing, which is safe precisely because it was never load-bearing.

3.2 Popover

The tooltip's interactive inverse, and the last surface in the book. A light, anchored, non-blocking panel that holds what the tooltip refuses — links, buttons, a small form, a menu. It sits between the two extremes it borrows from: it carries interactive content like a modal, but it does not block like one; it anchors to a trigger like a tooltip, but it does not hide on the way to it. Getting a component this in-between right is a matter of holding two boundaries at once, and that is the whole of this section.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A small surface, opened on purpose, holding content the user interacts with while the page stays live behind it. It is deliberate where the tooltip is passive, and it is dismissable where the modal is blocking.

Use a popover whenUse something else when
The anchored content is interactive — a menu of actions, a filter, a small form, a linkThe content is a passive label or hint → Tooltip
The user opens it deliberately and the page can stay usable underneathThe decision must be made before anything else and must not be lost → Modal
Losing it to a click outside is harmless — they can reopen itA stray click outside would discard work or a choice that matters → Modal
The content is small and self-containedIt grows into a full form or a multi-step flow → a page or a Drawer

Both boundaries are about dismissal. Downward: a popover is interactive, so it is click-triggered — you cannot ask a user to travel a mouse across a gap to reach content that a tooltip's hover would close. Upward: a popover is light-dismissed — Escape or a click anywhere outside closes it — so it must never hold a decision the user would be hurt to lose to a stray click. Content the user opens and uses: popover. A choice they must deliberately make: modal. The click-to-open keeps it clear of the tooltip; the light-dismiss keeps it clear of the modal.

5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST open on click or key press, never on hover. Interactive content cannot sit behind a hover state — the pointer cannot reach it without crossing a gap that closes it. Hover belongs to the tooltip.
  2. MUST wire the trigger: aria-expanded tracks open state, aria-haspopup names the type (dialog or menu), aria-controls points at the popover.
  3. MUST move focus into the popover on open — to the first focusable element — and return it to the trigger on close. MUST NOT trap focus: a popover is not a modal. Tabbing past the last element moves focus onward and closes the popover; the page behind is never sealed off.
  4. MUST light-dismiss: Escape closes and returns focus; a click or tap outside closes. Because of this, MUST NOT hold a decision that must not be lost — a destructive confirm, unsaved input the user cannot recover. That is a modal.
  5. MUST NOT render a scrim or block the page. The page stays interactive underneath; the popover is a surface on top, not a gate in front.
  6. MUST carry the role its content implies — role="dialog" (non-modal, no aria-modal="true") for a form or free content, role="menu" with menuitems for an actions list — and a label. A generic div of buttons is not a menu.
  7. MUST keep the surface neutral and the frame free of state tint; a state that matters inside is an inline alert. The only coloured element is a destructive item's text, via --action-danger.
  8. MUST stay in the viewport by flipping and shifting — the same collision behaviour as the tooltip — and keep interactive content fully on-screen and reachable.

6 · Behaviour

  • Open. The trigger is pressed; the popover opens on the preferred side and focus moves to its first control. A second press, or pressing a different trigger, closes it.
  • Positioning and collision. The same engine as the tooltip: preferred side, flip when it would overflow, shift along the cross-axis, arrow tracking the trigger. Anchored surfaces share this so their behaviour is identical whichever one is open.
  • Dismissal. Escape, an outside click or tap, choosing a menu item, or completing the form's action — all close it and return focus to the trigger. There is no modal-style close button unless the content is large enough to warrant one.
  • Focus. In on open, onward-not-trapped while open, back to the trigger on close. This is the single behavioural line between a popover and a modal, and it is the one most often got wrong by building a popover on a modal's focus trap.
  • Nesting. One popover per trigger; a popover does not spawn another except as a true menu submenu, handled by the menu pattern. A popover that opens a popover that opens a popover is a flow that wanted a page.
  • Motion. A quick fade with a small scale or translate from the anchor; under prefers-reduced-motion, fade only.
