0.1 How to read this book

Every component section has the same eight parts, in the same order. If you are implementing, you can read parts 3, 5 and 8 and ignore the rest.

#PartWhat it is for
1Purpose and boundaryWhat it is, and which component to use instead when it is the wrong one. Boundaries are why we ended up with three modal systems.
2AnatomyNamed parts. The names are the class names.
3Token map by roleEvery part, its token, what it resolves to, and its measured contrast against its own surface. Normative.
4Required statesVariants and interaction states. If it is listed here, shipping without it is incomplete, not minimal.
5Accessibility — hard rulesNumbered MUST / MUST NOT. Each one is testable by a human in under ten seconds.
6BehaviourTiming, stacking, dismissal, focus movement, announcement.
7Rendered exampleLive, in this page, using only tokens. If it renders here it is buildable anywhere.
8Markup contractThe DOM that Vue 2 and React must both produce. This is what makes the spec framework-agnostic and what makes conformance greppable.
Why a rendered example and a markup contract, rather than a component library

A library commits to a framework and dates immediately — Unity is Vue 2, Scale targets React. A contract does not. Both implementations produce the same DOM and consume the same tokens, so a tenant stylesheet, a QA script and a designer's eye all target one thing. The library follows the contract; the contract does not follow the library.

0.2 The four laws

Four rules govern every component in this book. Everything downstream is application. Laws 1 and 2 are locked outputs of the conformance audit. Laws 3 and 4 are what the audit numbers force once you try to apply 1 and 2 to real components.

Law 1
Fill, text and border are different steps of one colour. A hex is never reused across roles.

One value cannot satisfy three contrast obligations at once. The audit found the current skins using the same state hex as background 1,289 times, as text 1,193 times and as border 593 times — which is why warning sits at 1.81:1 and success at 3.39:1.

Accent step 11 is hand-corrected to #0d5266. Verified in OKLCH: L 0.409, C 0.071, H 223.1° — between step 10 (L 0.453) and step 12 (L 0.351), hue drift under 1°, chroma tapering correctly toward 12. Monotonic. 8.69:1 on white, up from 5.25:1. The generator produced a lighter step 11 than step 9 because #0B6E8A is an unusually dark step 9 anchor; the ramp is now documented as hand-tuned, not generator output.

A second hand-correction exists, for the same class of reason in the opposite direction: warning step 7 (#eeb95f). Amber's anchor is unusually light, so a generated step 7 lands at 1.23:1 against its own step 3 and is invisible as a border. Both corrections belong in the ramp documentation as anchor-outlier fixes.

Law 2
A state is a set of roles, not a colour. It resolves to four ramp positions from one hue, contrast-correct by construction.

Surface, border, icon, text. Four roles, one hue, no judgement calls at the call site. A developer choosing a state never chooses a value.

Law 3
Contrast is a property of a pair, not of a step. Every token declares the surface it is legal against.

This is the correction that makes Law 1 survive contact with a tinted surface. "Step 11 is accessible text" is true on white and false on a state surface: on step 3, the three state step-11s measure 4.33, 4.05 and 4.42:1 — all under AA. On a tinted surface, text moves to step 12. See 0.5.

The same applies to borders. Accent step 7 on white is 1.80:1, so it is a legal container hairline and an illegal focus ring or sole state indicator, both of which owe 3:1.

Law 4
Step 9 has no role in a state-bearing surface. There is no solid state fill.

White text on step 9 measures 3.87:1 on error, 3.15:1 on success and 2.04:1 on warning. Three of four fail AA. Making solid state surfaces safe requires a per-hue on-fill token, which reintroduces exactly the judgement call Law 2 removes — and the audit shows what happens to judgement calls at 62,235 call sites.

So: no solid alerts, no solid toasts, no solid banners. Step 9 stays where it earns its contrast — accent fills and primary actions on white. Hue in the state group is carried by surface, border and icon.

Status and state are different things and must not share a namespace

Scale already ships --status-active-*, --status-pending-*, --status-cancelled-*. Those describe a property of a record — a job is on hold, a review is pending. They persist, they are filterable, they belong to data.

This book's --state-* tokens describe a system response to an event — the save failed, the export finished. They are ephemeral and belong to the interface.

They currently collide on hue: green means both "complete" and "success", amber means both "pending" and "warning". That collision is survivable, but sharing a token namespace is not — it guarantees someone eventually styles a toast with --status-cancelled-bg and nobody catches it in review. Keep the two vocabularies separate and the ambiguity stays visible.

0.3 Token layers and reach

Three layers. The rule that matters is not what each layer contains, it is how far into the stack each layer is allowed to reach.

LayerContainsMay referenceMay be referenced by
1 · Primitive--accent-9, --amber-11Nothing. Literal values.Layer 2 only.
2 · Semantic--state-warning-icon, --text-defaultLayer 1.Layer 3, and application CSS.
3 · Component--alert-surface, --modal-backdropLayer 2. Never layer 1.The component's own rules only.

A component stylesheet that references a primitive is non-conformant, even when the value is right. This is the single check with the highest leverage in the book: it is one grep, it has no false negatives, and it catches the failure mode the audit found in the chatbot widget — a step 11 value pinned into the step 9 slot, where the instinct was correct, the slot was wrong, and the review passed because the value looked reasonable.

Layer 3 is not optional decoration

Variants set component tokens and nothing else. The alert has one background declaration in the entire component; the four variants each set four custom properties. That is what makes a fifth state a four-line change instead of a new rule block, and it is why the audit's finding that no overlay has any state variant is a cheap fix rather than a rewrite — once the layer exists.

0.4 The role model

Four roles. They are named for what they colour, not for the CSS property they land in, because -bg and -fg are the names that let one value do three jobs.

RoleStepObligationApplies to
surface3None — it is the reference, not the referentbackground
border71.5–1.7:1 against its own surface. Decorative: it may not be the sole carrier of meaning, so it owes no 3:1.border-color, dividers
icon11≥3:1 against its own surface (WCAG 1.4.11 — it is a carrier of meaning)glyph color, fill
text12≥4.5:1 against its own surface — in practice ≥9.9:1body color, links

Note what is absent. There is no fill role in this group, per Law 4. And step 12 as text is not a compromise, it is the point: hue is carried by the surface, border and icon, so the text is free to be a hue-tinted near-black and simply be readable. Every state, every theme, no exceptions to remember.

0.7 Conformance method

Every component section ends with checks that a script can run. This is the part that closes the loop: the audit that produced this book took six scripted passes over 890,000 lines. The next one should be a CI job.

A rule that cannot be grepped is a preference. If a rule in this book has no check, either it needs one or it does not belong in the book.

CheckPassing result
Layer violationNo component stylesheet references a layer 1 primitive.
Literal violationNo hex literal from 0.5 appears outside the token declaration block.
Role violationNo --state-*-surface in a color:; no --state-*-text in a background:.
Contract violationEvery .sd-alert carries data-state. Every dismiss control has an accessible name.
AdoptionCount of legacy calls remaining. Conformance and adoption are different numbers and are reported separately.
Conformance is not adoption

The audit's central finding was that sd-modal is used 5 times against 1,484 legacy calls. A component can be perfectly conformant and reach nobody. Every section reports both numbers, because the remediation for each is completely different: conformance is a pull request, adoption is a programme.

1.1 Inline alert

The base component of the state-bearing group. Toast and banner are this plus ephemerality and this plus persistence — so this is where the state model is settled.

1 · Purpose and boundary

A message bound to a region of the page, in the document flow, persisting until the condition that caused it changes. It does not move, it does not time out, and it does not take focus.

Use an inline alert whenUse something else when
The message is about a specific region and belongs beside itThe message is about a background operation the user has moved on from → Toast
The user must still be able to see and act on the underlying contentThe message is about the whole application or account and outlives the page → Banner
The condition persists until something changes itThe message blocks progress and needs a decision now → Modal
The message can carry a link or a secondary actionThe message is about one specific field → field-level validation, not this

Field-level validation is the boundary most often crossed. An alert summarising six invalid fields is correct; an alert standing in for the message under the field is not, and it costs the user the pointer to where the problem is.

Two decisions in that table are worth stating out loud.

Links and actions take body colour, not icon colour. Every instinct says a link should be the accent or the state hue. On a tinted surface both fail: step 11 lands at 4.05:1 on warning. The link is distinguished by underline and weight, which is a stronger affordance than hue anyway and survives the greyscale test.

Dismiss hover is a neutral alpha overlay, not a state step. Using a state step would need four more tokens and four more measurements to add nothing. The overlay works identically on all four surfaces and on any fifth we add later.

Known asymmetry — the info icon is darker than the other three

Info's icon resolves to corrected accent step 11 at 7.77:1, against 4.05–4.42:1 for the other three, so it reads visually heavier. This is downstream of the same dark anchor that broke the ramp. Using accent step 9 for the info icon would balance it at 5.20:1 — but it puts step 9 back in the state group and reopens Law 4 for one glyph's sake. Recommendation: keep step 11 and accept the asymmetry. Flagged so it is recognised as a known trade rather than rediscovered as a bug.

4 · Required states

VariantAttributeMeaningAnnouncement
Errordata-state="error"The operation failed. Something is broken now.assertive
Warningdata-state="warning"The operation will succeed with a consequence, or is about to fail.assertive
Successdata-state="success"The operation completed. Only where completion is not otherwise visible.polite
Infodata-state="info"Context the user did not ask for and needs anyway. The default.polite

Interaction states: default; dismiss hover; dismiss active; :focus-visible on dismiss and on each action; and --quiet, which drops the border where alerts stack inside an already-bordered container. There is no disabled alert — an alert that should not be read should not be rendered.

5 · Accessibility — hard rules

  1. MUST resolve every colour through --alert-*. A variant sets four custom properties and declares no other colour. MUST NOT reference a layer 1 primitive anywhere in component CSS.
  2. MUST carry an icon and a text message. Hue is never the sole carrier of state (WCAG 1.4.1). The alert must survive a greyscale screenshot.
  3. MUST mark the icon aria-hidden="true" and convey state in text — either in the copy or in a visually hidden prefix. A screen reader user gets "Error:", not a described glyph.
  4. MUST use step 12 for all text on the alert surface. MUST NOT use step 11 for body copy, links or action labels. 4.05:1 fails AA.
  5. MUST NOT render a solid state fill or place white text on any state step 9. Law 4.
  6. MUST announce dynamically inserted alerts via a live region: role="alert" for error and warning, role="status" for success and info. MUST NOT put a live region on an alert that is present at page load — it is read in document order, and a live region makes it interrupt or duplicate.
  7. MUST keep the live region container in the DOM before the message is inserted. A live region added and populated in the same tick is not announced in most screen readers.
  8. MUST keep the focus ring at 2px accent-9, 2px offset, on :focus-visible. MUST NOT replace it with a background change — the offset ring is the only indicator that clears 3:1 on all four tinted surfaces.
  9. MUST NOT place a filled button inside an alert. Actions are underlined text buttons. A filled button needs an on-fill colour, which reopens Law 4, and it competes with the page's one primary action.
  10. MUST NOT offer dismissal on an error whose condition is unresolved. Dismissing an alert the user cannot act on hides a live problem and leaves no way to recover it. Errors bound to a persistent condition are not dismissible.
  11. MUST give the dismiss control a specific accessible name — "Dismiss error message", not "Close" — and a 44×44 interactive area via pseudo-element, not padding.
  12. MUST move focus to a sensible destination when a focused alert is removed: the triggering control if it still exists, otherwise the container heading. MUST NOT let focus fall to <body>.

6 · Behaviour

  • Lifetime. Until the condition changes. No timers.
  • Placement. Directly above the region it describes, inside that region's bounds. A form alert sits inside the form.
  • Stacking. Maximum two per region. A third means the messages should be one alert with a list.
  • Replacement. A new alert in the same slot replaces rather than appends, and re-announces.
  • Entry. 150ms opacity fade. No slide, no height animation — inline alerts change layout, and animating that costs a reflow and a jump under the pointer.

8 · Markup contract

Both implementations produce this DOM. Nothing below is framework-specific.

Canonical DOM
<div class="sd-alert" data-state="error" role="alert"> <svg class="sd-alert__icon" aria-hidden="true">…</svg> <div class="sd-alert__body"> <p class="sd-alert__title"><span class="sr-only">Error: </span>Title</p> <p class="sd-alert__message">Message</p> <div class="sd-alert__actions"> <button type="button" class="sd-alert__action">Action</button> </div> </div> <button type="button" class="sd-alert__dismiss" aria-label="Dismiss error message">…</button> </div> role: "alert" for error|warning, "status" for success|info, omitted entirely when present at page load.

Why data-state and not a modifier class

A class is additive: sd-alert-error sd-alert-warning is valid HTML and renders whichever rule wins the cascade. An attribute holds one value. The state becomes an enumerable, greppable, mutually exclusive property that a linter can validate against a list of four — and it prevents the specific defect the audit found, where the overlay system has no state layer and the toast classes that do exist are applied without ever being styled.

1.2 Toast

Not yet written — awaiting approval of 0.x and 1.1

Inherits the entire state model from 1.1 and adds: ephemerality and timing, portal and stacking order, live-region politeness under rapid succession, pause-on-hover and pause-on-focus, and the interaction with prefers-reduced-motion. Audit dependencies: three toast classes are applied and never styled, so the current state colour is vue-toasted's vendor default; and two sites call Vue.toasted.show directly, bypassing the wrapper — so the wrapper is not a chokepoint and the contract cannot assume it is.

1.3 Banner

Not yet written — awaiting approval of 0.x and 1.1

Inherits the state model and adds: full-bleed layout at zero radius, persistence across navigation, precedence when more than one qualifies, and the relationship to the left nav rail and page chrome. No banner component exists in sdcore/src/pattern/ — this is a build. Open question this section must answer: whether a banner may ever be solid, given Law 4. Current position is no.
